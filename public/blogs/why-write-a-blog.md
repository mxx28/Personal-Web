---
title: Why Write a Blog
date: "2026-04-29"
description: "Why writing blogs quietly changed the way I learn."
---

For a long time, writing blogs felt like something that had nothing to do with me.

I was always the one reading other people’s posts.
Whenever I got stuck, I searched, found a well-written explanation, saved it, maybe gave it a like, and moved on.

But I never really thought I would be the one writing.

And honestly, in a world where large language models are everywhere now, it even feels like fewer people seriously read technical blogs anymore. Most of the time, it’s faster to just ask a model and get an answer, instead of slowly working through someone else’s thinking.

So before I actually started writing, I kept wondering:
what do I really get out of this? does it even matter?

---

What changed my mind wasn’t some big, inspiring reason.

It was something small.

I once tried to write down something I had just learned. I remember thinking it would be easy—almost trivial, like “there’s nothing to even explain here.”

And then I got stuck.

Not slightly stuck. Completely stuck.

I just… couldn’t continue.

And that moment was honestly a bit uncomfortable.

Because up until then, I had assumed I understood it. The code worked, the results made sense, and I could say a few things about it if someone asked.

But when I actually tried to explain it clearly, everything fell apart.

It felt like I only had fragments.
Pieces of understanding, but nothing that really held together.

---

That’s when I started realizing something:
a lot of the time, when we say “I understand this,” what we really mean is “I’ve seen this before.”

Real understanding is different.
It’s when you can explain something from start to finish, to someone who doesn’t already know it.

There’s a well-known idea from Richard Feynman—if you truly understand something, you should be able to explain it in simple terms.

It sounds obvious. But actually trying to do it is hard.

Because we rely so much on things that *feel* like understanding.

Terminology. привычные patterns of explanation. assumptions we don’t even notice we’re making. You say things like “attention,” “embedding,” “distribution shift,” and it almost feels like the explanation is already complete.

But what if the other person doesn’t know any of those words?

Can you still explain it?

---

Writing a blog forces you into that situation.

You have to take everything that felt “obvious” and unpack it again. Step by step. What actually happens? What matters? Where did I skip something?

And it’s kind of unforgiving.

Because you can’t fake it.
The parts you can’t write are exactly the parts you don’t understand.

But that’s also when I started seeing writing differently.

It stopped feeling like a way to “show what I know,” and started feeling more like a way to test it.

Every time you write, you’re turning something vague into something clear.

And over time, that changes how you learn.

Before, everything felt fragmented. A bit of this today, something else tomorrow. It felt like I knew a lot, but it didn’t really connect.

Once you start writing, you naturally begin looking for structure. You start asking: what’s the actual thread here? what am I still unclear about?

And slowly, you move from just “knowing things” to actually understanding them.

---

Another thing that surprised me is this:
you shouldn’t wait until you fully understand something before writing.

Because that moment probably never comes.

The most valuable parts are often when you *almost* understand something—when you’re still unsure, still correcting yourself, still figuring things out.

That process is where the real thinking happens.

If you wait until everything is perfectly clean and polished, there’s often nothing left to say.

And there’s also a very practical reason.

Things you feel completely clear about right now—you will forget them.

I sometimes go back and read things I wrote before. And it’s interesting.

Sometimes I think, “oh, that’s how I saw it back then.”
Sometimes I notice things I didn’t fully understand at the time.

It feels a bit like looking at an earlier version of yourself.

---

So at some point, I stopped worrying about whether something is “worth writing.”

If I understand something a little better than before, I write it down.

At this point, writing blogs doesn’t feel like “producing content” anymore.

It just feels like part of learning.

Maybe even the most important part.

If I had to put it in one sentence, it would be this—

When you can truly explain something clearly, that’s when it actually becomes yours.
